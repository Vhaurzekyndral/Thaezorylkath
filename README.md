# Thaezorylkath

> A crown of drowned bells waits for the tide that will return its nameless sovereign.

## The Harbor With No Reflections

The harbor's water reflects neither boats nor sky. It shows a ceiling of dark stone crossed by pale roots. At low tide, those roots appear closer. At high tide, small lights move between them like windows passing through a tunnel.

The harbor master keeps a crown beneath his desk. It is made from little bells joined by black wire. Each bell contains a grain of white sand instead of a clapper. When the crown is lifted, the sand remains where it was, suspended in the air until the bells are lowered around it again.

He raises it only when a ship arrives without a name. These ships carry no cargo that can be weighed. Their captains pay in photographs of coastlines and leave before anyone can ask where the pictures were taken.

## A Ship With a Single Chair

One evening, a narrow ship entered carrying a chair on its deck. Its captain was a woman with a silver mark across her palm. She asked the harbor master for a berth facing inland.

He showed her the crown. One bell began to ring, though its grain of sand had not moved. In the harbor water, a window appeared among the roots. Behind it stood a chair identical to the one on deck.

The woman touched the silver mark. She remembered sitting in a room while someone measured her hand with a length of dark thread. She had been told to keep still until the last bell sounded.

She did not remember hearing a first bell. She did not remember leaving the room. Yet every ship she had captained since had carried the chair, and every port had asked why its shadow faced the wrong way.

## The Mooring Beneath the Desk

The harbor master tied the ship to a ring beneath his desk. The rope passed through the office floor without leaving a hole. Outside, the vessel settled against the quay as if it had reached a familiar shore.

He poured tea into two cups. A third appeared in the crown's reflection on the polished desk. Beside it rested a hand marked with silver, larger than the captain's and covered in overlapping scales.

The captain placed her chair opposite his. Its shadow entered the desk's reflection and sat beside the third cup. The bell stopped ringing. From the harbor came a sound like a great door opening beneath water.

The roots in the water parted, revealing a staircase whose steps were made of shells. At its lowest landing hung an empty coat, moving gently in a current nobody above could feel.

## The Shape Beneath the Quay

The crown had been dredged from the harbor before the office was built. Beneath it, the workers found a scale larger than their boat. Beneath the scale, an eye opened just long enough to show them a quay with no harbor beside it.

The harbor master was the only worker who remained. He built his office above the eye and kept the bells where the creature might hear them through the floor.

He called it a sovereign because every ship seemed to arrive within the reach of its sleeping hand. Its wings lay beneath the town. Its horns supported the stone ceiling visible in the water. The roots belonged to trees growing in a country above that ceiling, where no tide had ever been recorded.

The captain asked whether it was waiting to wake. He answered by turning the crown toward her chair.

## A Tide Written in Wire

The black wire had acquired four marks:

*Thae — zoryl — kath — umven.*

She spoke them into the smallest bell. The white sand became clear, each grain holding a tiny harbor with a ship at rest. The silver mark on her palm opened like a narrow window.

Through it, she saw the measured hand from her memory. The dark thread ran from its fingers to the crown. Beyond it sat the scaled sovereign, its head lowered beside an empty chair.

She understood none of what she saw well enough to explain it. She placed her palm against the desk, and the third cup in the reflection filled with light.

Outside, the nameless ship turned slowly toward the sea. Its mooring rope remained tied, but no longer seemed to require distance between its ends.

## The Berth Facing Inland

By morning, the ship had gone. The chair remained in the office. Its shadow now lay beneath it, ordinary and quiet. The harbor master found a photograph on the seat showing the quay from far inland, with a pale forest growing where the water should have been.

He hung it beside his window. At high tide, the trees in the photograph bend toward the harbor, and the crown's bells grow warm beneath the desk.

The captain has not returned. Some nights, however, a silver light passes through the roots below the water, pauses beneath the office, and continues toward the open sea.

The harbor still has no reflections.

But one empty berth faces inland, and beneath it an enormous hand holds a grain of sand without closing its claws.
